Gout treatment
For every line you read, ask: is this to stop the flare now, or to lower uric acid for life?
Two separate arms, each with its own non-pharmacological and pharmacological parts.

Arm 1: treat the acute flare

Section 6 · goal: fast relief of pain and inflammation · start within 24 hours · short course (days)

6.1 Non-pharmacological (helper only, not enough alone)

  • Ice packs: the most effective non-drug measure (conditional recommendation)
  • Rest the joint: immobilisation speeds recovery

6.2 Pharmacological (choose by patient factors; “medication-in-pocket” for early use)

First-line6.2.1 Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: naproxen, indomethacin, sulindac (approved for gout); high dose, shortest course, until 24 hours after symptoms settle. Avoid: peptic ulcer risk, anticoagulants, renal impairment, uncontrolled hypertension, heart failure. Add a proton pump inhibitor to protect the stomach.
First-line6.2.2 Colchicine: low dose only, 1.2 mg then 0.6 mg one hour later; must start within 36 hours. Not a painkiller; blocks neutrophils. Diarrhoea and nausea warn of toxicity. Caution: renal impairment, interacting drugs (clarithromycin, verapamil, ritonavir, ciclosporin).
First-line6.2.3 Glucocorticoids: oral prednisone or prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg/day for 5 to 10 days; intra-articular for one or two large joints (infection excluded first); intramuscular or intravenous if the patient cannot swallow. Best choice in renal impairment. Watch: blood glucose in diabetes.
Not first-line6.2.4 Corticotropin: works, but expensive and not approved for gout.
If first-line fails6.2.5 Interleukin-1 inhibitors: canakinumab (approved 2023) or anakinra (off-label); subcutaneous; main side effect is injection-site reaction.
Severe6.2.6 Combination: severe polyarticular attack, e.g. colchicine plus an anti-inflammatory drug or oral steroid.
Opioids have little role: gout pain comes from inflammation, so treat the inflammation.

Arm 2: lower uric acid for life

Section 7 · goal: serum uric acid below 6 mg/dL so crystals dissolve and flares stop · long term (years)

7.1 Who needs it?

Strong2 or more flares a year · radiographic damage · any tophus
ConditionalMore than one flare but fewer than 2 a year · first flare plus chronic kidney disease stage 3 or worse, uric acid above 9 mg/dL, or kidney stones
Not recommendedFirst flare alone · asymptomatic hyperuricaemia (24 patients treated for 3 years to prevent one flare)

7.2 Target and 7.3 timing

  • Target: below 6 mg/dL (357 µmol/L); do not keep it below 3 mg/dL long term
  • Treat-to-target: measure uric acid and raise the dose until the target is reached (weeks to months); nurse or pharmacist can lead
  • Start: can begin during a flare, once anti-inflammatory treatment has started
  • Never stop it during a flare; continue indefinitely

7.4 Non-pharmacological (not enough alone)

  • Lifestyle: lose weight, exercise, limit alcohol, purines and high-fructose corn syrup; stay hydrated; no vitamin C supplements
  • Medication review: switch hydrochlorothiazide; keep low-dose aspirin and statins
Trap: the lecture lists losartan (preferred), fenofibrate (not recommended) and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (may help) here. They are drugs, chosen for other diseases the patient has.

7.5 Pharmacological

7.5.1 CoverProphylaxis when starting: low-dose colchicine, naproxen or prednisone for 3 to 6 months, because starting therapy can trigger flares
7.5.2 First-lineAllopurinol: start 100 mg or less (50 mg in kidney disease), raise every 2 to 5 weeks, maximum 800 mg. Test HLA-B*58:01 in Southeast Asian and African American patients. Rare but fatal hypersensitivity syndrome. Febuxostat: alternative, 40 then 80 mg; cardiovascular death warning.
7.5.3 AlternativeProbenecid: increases uric acid excretion in urine. Avoid: kidney disease stage 3 or worse, kidney stones. Lesinurad withdrawn 2019.
7.5.4 Last resortPegloticase: 8 mg intravenous every 2 weeks; refractory gout only. Screen for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency.

Why the same drugs appear twice: same drug, different job

Arm 1 treats a flare at full dose for days. Arm 2 prevents flares at low dose for 3 to 6 months after urate-lowering therapy starts.
